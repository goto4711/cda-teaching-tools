Term frequency: guide

How a text becomes a row of numbers · Week 3 (preview) and Week 4: Cultural Data Forms: Text

Before a computer can compare, search or classify texts, it has to turn them into numbers. The simplest way is to count words: the bag of words. This tool does it for three short documents you can edit, weights the counts with TF-IDF, and measures how similar the documents then look. You can see exactly what survives the counting and what is lost.

2. What the tool does with your text

  1. Lower case. “The” and “the” become the same word.
  2. Split into words. Anything that is not a letter, digit or underscore separates words, so punctuation disappears and “don't” becomes two words, “don” and “t”.
  3. Remove stopwords. Every word in the stopword list is dropped.
  4. Build the vocabulary. All remaining words from all three documents, each once, sorted.
  5. Count. For each document, how often each vocabulary word occurs.
  6. Weight (TF-IDF mode). count × ln(3 / df), where df is the number of documents that contain the word. This is the “usual textbook version” from the Week 4 code-along. scikit-learn's TfidfVectorizer uses a smoothed variant, so its numbers differ slightly.
  7. Compare. Cosine similarity between every pair of rows.

There is no stemming: “jump” and “jumps” are different words. In Python, steps 1–5 are what CountVectorizer does.

  1. Ask the room to compare rows 1 and 3 before you point it out. They are identical, although in one the fox jumps over the dog and in the other the dog jumps over the fox.
  2. Scroll to the similarity table: Doc 1 and Doc 3 score 1.00; each scores 0.41 with Doc 2.
  3. Find “jump” and “jumps”: the computer sees two unrelated words.
  4. “over” is struck through as a stopword. Is it really meaningless here?

4. Walkthrough 2: who bites whom?

Open with the example

Dog bites man.100110
Man bites dog.100110
The man was bitten by the dog.011111

The two sentences that mean opposite things get the same vector (similarity 1.00). The two that mean the same thing (“dog bites man” and “the man was bitten by the dog”) share only two words. Counting words keeps what a text is about and loses who does what.

5. Walkthrough 3: stopwords

  1. With the fox example, empty the stopword box. The struck-through words come back: the vocabulary grows from 8 to 11 words, and “the” counts 2 in Document 1.
  2. Now add dog to the stopwords. The one word all three documents share disappears.
  3. Ask: who decides what counts as a stopword? For a corpus of legal texts, would “shall” be one? For poetry, would “the”?

6. Walkthrough 4: TF-IDF

Open the fox example in TF-IDF  press Space to switch back and forth

  1. Look at the two extra rows. “dog” and “lazy” are in all three documents, so df = 3 and idf = ln(3/3) = 0: they vanish, however often they occur.
  2. “jump” and “never” are only in Doc 2 (df = 1), so they get the highest weight, 1.10.
  3. Look at the similarity table. Doc 2 was 0.41 similar to the others with counts; with TF-IDF it drops to 0.00, because the words it shared were exactly the common ones.
  4. Doc 1 and Doc 3 are still identical. Weighting changes how much each word counts, not the fact that word order is gone.

TF-IDF rewards words that are frequent in one document and rare in the others: the words that make a document distinctive. That is why the State of the Union exercise uses TF-IDF to find each speech's top words: words that every speech uses score low.

7. Walkthrough 5: house and garden, as in the code-along

Open with the Week 4 documents

These are the three documents and the stopword list from the Week 4 code-along, so students can check their Python output against the tool.

  1. With counts, Doc 1 (the beautiful garden) is most like Doc 3 (the green garden): 0.41. Doc 2 (the red roof) is least like Doc 3: 0.17.
  2. Switch to TF-IDF. “house” is in all three documents and drops to 0, and all three similarities fall to 0.05 or below.
  3. Ask: all three documents are about a house. Is a weighting that removes “house” a good one here? What changes when there are 230 speeches instead of 3 documents?

8. Questions for class

  1. What does a bag of words keep, and what does it throw away? Name one research question for which that is fine, and one for which it is not.
  2. The State of the Union corpus in the code-along has thousands of different words. How many columns would its table have? How many of the cells would be 0?
  3. Why might a historian not want to remove stopwords?
  4. “Dog” and “canine” never count as similar here. What would a representation need to know to see that they are? (Try the embeddings demonstration.)
